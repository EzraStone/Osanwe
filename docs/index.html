Who can learn what

There is no single participant called “the service.”

Each role receives different information. The design matters only when relay and gateway are actually operated separately, and even then timing, collusion, and identifying prompt content remain.

ParticipantWhat it seesWhat is kept
Local clientYour request, because your own application sends it thereCounters and connection state; no server-side conversation log
RelayYour network address and encrypted flow—ciphertext, not prompt textNo protocol decryption key for the request
GatewayPrompt and answer text; attaches its provider credentialNot your source network address
Model providerPrompt, answer, and timing; your account in bring-your-own-key modeUnknown unless a dated provider policy is shown
MintThat an entitlement was redeemed; signs it blindlyNot the credential later shown to the gateway

What we can say today

  • A correctly configured and pinned relay receives end-to-end ciphertext and has no protocol decryption key for the request.
  • Blind-signed tokens cannot be directly cryptographically matched to their blinded issuance transcript.
  • The local process exposes no server-side conversation-history endpoint.

What we cannot say today

  • That the gateway operator is technically unable to read prompts.
  • That writing style or prompt content cannot identify a person.
  • That every provider deletes or declines to train on prompts.
Current status

The client, pinned relay, blind-token gateway, durable replay protection, aggregate limits, Windows installer, and cross-platform archives are built and tested. Public use remains gated by an independently operated relay, complete per-invite wallet, provider permission, platform validation, and independent review of the payment path.

One request, divided knowledge

The privacy design depends on separation, not a reassuring label.

Each component learns something. When relay and gateway actually have separate operators, this experimental design aims to prevent one operator from directly joining network identity, content, and provider account. Timing analysis or collusion can still link them.

  1. Local app

    Sees the conversation and keeps history ephemeral unless you explicitly save it in this browser.

  2. Relay

    Sees source address, timing, volume, and allowed destination—but carries encrypted content.

  3. Gateway

    Sees plaintext and a blind-signed token, but is not sent the relay-side network identity.

  4. Model provider

    Sees content under the gateway account, subject to its own retention and training policy.

No component sees nothing.

The design becomes meaningful only when relay and gateway have separate operators. Even then, prompt details, timing, collusion, and provider policy remain real ways information can be learned or linked. Osanwë exposes that boundary instead of replacing it with a privacy score.

Privacy boundary

What Osanwë does—and does not—hide.

Writing style, facts inside a prompt, endpoint timing, a colluding relay and gateway, and the provider's own data handling remain meaningful risks.

Osanwë product conviction The interior life is not raw material. This is not a claim that the prototype already provides perfect anonymity.
